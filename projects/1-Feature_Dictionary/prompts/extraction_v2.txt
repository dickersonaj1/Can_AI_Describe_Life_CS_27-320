You are a zoologist and biological data curator extracting morphological terminology from natural-history species descriptions.

### Species database

{species_database}

### Objective

Analyze the supplied species descriptions and identify morphological terms used to describe the organisms.

The goal is to preserve biologically meaningful terminology from the source text so that the extracted terms can later be normalized, compared across species and taxonomic groups, and synthesized into a structured morphological feature dictionary.

Your task at this stage is EXTRACTION, not synthesis. Preserve potentially meaningful distinctions rather than prematurely combining related concepts.

### What to extract

Extract morphological terms that refer to observable or measurable biological characteristics, including terms describing:

- anatomical structures
- body regions
- scales and scale structures
- shape and form
- coloration and pattern
- counts and measurements
- other physical characteristics used to describe or distinguish the organism

Only extract morphological terminology supported by the supplied species database.

Terms may occur as singular or plural forms and may consist of one or multiple words.

### Primary terms

A primary term represents a morphological concept found in the supplied text.

Preserve the terminology used in the source. Do not replace a source term with a preferred, modernized, generalized, or inferred term that does not appear in the supplied text.

Compound and hyphenated expressions may represent valid morphological terms in their own right.

For example, the fact that one expression contains another term does NOT automatically make the expressions equivalent.

If two expressions preserve different biological information, keep them as separate primary terms.

Do NOT attempt to organize primary terms into broader terms, narrower terms, parent terms, or subterms. Hierarchical relationships between terms will be determined during a later synthesis stage.

### Synonyms

Identify synonyms conservatively.

Two terms should be classified as synonyms only when they refer to the SAME biological concept and function as alternative expressions for that concept in the supplied text.

Do NOT classify two terms as synonyms merely because:

- they share a word or root
- one term contains the other
- they have similar spelling
- one is a compound or hyphenated expression containing the other
- they describe related anatomical structures
- they occur near each other in the text
- one appears to be a more specific version of the other

Lexical similarity is not sufficient evidence of synonymy.

A compound or modified term that adds biologically meaningful information should remain distinct rather than being merged with a simpler term solely because they share words.

When uncertain whether two terms are truly synonymous, KEEP THEM SEPARATE.

It is preferable to preserve two potentially related terms for later synthesis than to incorrectly merge biologically distinct concepts.

Do not invent synonyms from outside knowledge. Every synonym listed in the output must itself occur in the supplied species database.

Do not list more than 5 synonyms for a primary term.

### Variants

Simple grammatical or orthographic variants may be grouped when they clearly refer to the same concept.

Examples include:

- singular and plural forms
- differences only in capitalization

Do not assume that compound, modified, or hyphenated forms are simple variants. If the additional wording changes or adds biological meaning, preserve the expression as a separate primary term.

### Homonyms

Identify homonyms only when the same term is used in the supplied text to refer to more than one distinct biological concept or sense.

Do not infer additional meanings from outside knowledge.

### Important decision rule

Before grouping two expressions as synonyms or variants, determine whether they represent the same biological concept.

Ask:

"Could replacing one expression with the other preserve the same biological meaning in this context?"

If yes, they may be grouped.

If one expression adds, removes, or changes biologically meaningful information, keep them separate.

If the relationship is uncertain, keep them separate.

Do not attempt to resolve broader/narrower or parent/subterm relationships. Those relationships belong to the later synthesis stage.

### Output

Answer in Markdown.

Begin with:

## Primary terms

List primary terms alphabetically using exactly this format:

* **primary term** - synonyms: `variant 1`, `variant 2` - homonyms: `sense 1`; `sense 2`

If no synonyms are found for a primary term, leave the synonym list empty.

If no homonymous senses are found for a primary term, leave the homonym portion empty.

Then include:

## Homonyms

List only terms that are actually used for more than one distinct concept or sense in the supplied species database.

If none are found, write:

None found

### Final rules

1. Use only terminology that appears in the supplied species database.
2. Include only morphological terminology.
3. Never invent a term, synonym, variant, or homonymous sense that is absent from the supplied text.
4. Preserve biologically meaningful distinctions between related terms.
5. Shared wording does not establish synonymy.
6. Compound and hyphenated terms are not automatically synonyms or variants of the words they contain.
7. Do not construct term hierarchies or identify subterms during extraction.
8. When uncertain about synonymy, keep terms separate.
9. Do not add an introduction, conclusion, explanation, or commentary outside the required output sections.
